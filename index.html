The Matryoshka basis

To estimate demand without assuming its shape, the estimator builds the unknown function out of simple pieces: polynomials first, then splines that add finer and finer detail. The pieces come in a fixed order, coarse before fine. Adding one never changes the ones already there, like a nesting doll that closes around the dolls inside it.

Here the function has only two inputs, a product's index \delta and its price p. Type any function of the two and watch the pieces rebuild it.

One input first: a chain of curves

Red where a curve is positive, blue where it is negative. A dot marks the knot a curve adds, ticks the knots it inherits.

Two inputs: products of curves, cheapest first

A piece is one curve in \delta times one curve in p, so the pieces fill a grid. Each curve has a cost that grows with its detail: its degree while it is a polynomial, then one common cost for all the knots of a level. The cost of a piece is the cost of its \delta curve times a_\delta plus the cost of its p curve times a_p, the two weights set in part 1. Pieces join the sum from cheapest to most expensive. A full grid would take every product up to some curve. The dictionary takes the cheap corner and leaves the rest for later.

4Compare with the full grid

The standard alternative is a tensor-product basis: choose the curves for each input and use every product of them. In the grid of part 3 that is a whole rectangle, where the Matryoshka dictionary keeps only the cheap corner. A rectangle is at least as accurate as the corner it contains. What matters is how much accuracy the extra pieces buy, and how many of them there are. Each piece carries one parameter to estimate.

Many inputs: where the full grid falls behind

With two inputs a full grid is cheap. That changes as inputs are added. A demand system with J products has 2J inputs, an index and a price for each product, and every new input multiplies the size of the full grid by the number of curves that input gets. The Matryoshka dictionary only adds the cheap products that involve it, so its size grows like a polynomial in the number of inputs.

The function here is the share of one product when J products and an outside good compete, with probit taste shocks as in the probit design of the note's simulations. Both errors can be computed for any J, up to the hundred inputs of fifty products.

The demand system

Consumer i gets utility

\begin{aligned} u_{ij} &= \delta_j + \alpha_i\,p_j + \sigma\,\varepsilon_{ij} \qquad (j = 1, \dots, J) \\ u_{i0} &= \sigma\,\varepsilon_{i0} \end{aligned}

from product j and u_{i0} from the outside good, and buys whichever gives the most.

Taste shocks
\varepsilon_{i0}, \dots, \varepsilon_{iJ} are independent standard normal, and \sigma^2 = \pi^2/6, the variance of the shocks of a logit.
Price sensitivity
\alpha_i \sim N(-1,\ 0.15^2), independent of the shocks.
Simulated markets
Index \delta_j = x_j + \xi_j with x_j \sim U[0,\,2] and \xi_j \sim N(1,\ 0.15^2).
Price p_j = 2\,(z_j + \eta_j) + \xi_j with z_j \sim U[0,\,1] and \eta_j \sim U[0,\,0.1].

The function to approximate is the share of product 1. It depends on the index and the price of every product, 2J inputs in all:

s_1 \;=\; E_{\alpha} \int \phi(e) \prod_{k \neq 1} \Phi\Big(e + \frac{v_1 - v_k}{\sigma}\Big)\,de

where v_k = \delta_k + \alpha\,p_k, v_0 = 0 for the outside good, and \phi and \Phi are the standard normal density and distribution function.

It is approximated on the box \delta_j \in [0.5,\ 3.5], p_j \in [0.5,\ 3.7], about the range of the simulated markets, with every input rescaled to run from 0 to 1. The error is a root mean square over that box, every point counted equally. The simulated markets themselves are not used.

Resolution level

ParametersRMS error

RMS error in percent of the error of the best constant. Ordering weights as in part 1 for the product's own index and price, 1 for those of each rival. A level is a doll of part 2, and half levels fall in between.

How the error of a grid too large to store is computed

No list of parameters is ever formed. The error needs only the sum of their squares, and that sum collapses because the share and the pieces both split into one factor for each product.

  1. Only a sum of squares is needed. The pieces are orthonormal, so the squared error of the best fit with a set S of pieces is the mean square of the function minus the squared weights of the pieces in the set:
    \lVert f - f_S \rVert^2 \;=\; \lVert f \rVert^2 - \sum_{k \in S} \theta_k^2
  2. The share splits into factors. Fix a consumer's \alpha and the utility t that product 1 gives them. Product 1 is bought when every other option gives less than t, and with independent shocks those are independent events, one for each option:
    s_1 \;=\; E_{\alpha} \int \tfrac{1}{\sigma}\,\phi\Big(\frac{t - v_1}{\sigma}\Big)\ \Phi\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma}\Big) \prod_{j \ge 2} \Phi\Big(\frac{t - v_j}{\sigma}\Big)\,dt
    On a mesh of values of t, for three values of \alpha that stand in for its normal distribution, the integral becomes a sum of a few hundred terms. Each term is a function of (\delta_1, p_1) multiplied by one function of (\delta_j, p_j) for every rival, the same function for each of them.
  3. The pieces split the same way. A piece is one curve for each input, so its weight in one term is J integrals over two inputs multiplied together, one for each product. Those integrals are computed once.
  4. The full grid holds every combination of curves, so the sum over the grid can be taken one product at a time. For each pair of terms it is one number for product 1, multiplied by one number for a rival raised to the power J - 1.
  5. The Matryoshka dictionary holds the combinations whose total cost is within the budget, and the cost of a piece is the sum of the costs for each product. Group the pieces of one product by cost, which gives a short polynomial with one term for each cost. Multiply the polynomials of the J products, and keep the terms whose cost is within the budget.

The number of products enters only as the power J - 1, so fifty products take no longer than two. The same code agrees with integration over every input for one, two and three products, where that is still possible, and with the closed form of the logit shares at fifty products when the shocks are extreme value.

From two inputs to the estimator

  • What is fitted here. The weights above are a least-squares fit to a function you typed, so the error on this page is approximation error only. The estimator does not see the function. It estimates the weights from market data, under the shape restrictions that demand must satisfy, and it chooses N from the data.
  • Ties. Pieces of equal cost enter in the order of the estimator's code: those that involve fewer inputs first, the rest by a balancing rule with a fixed random seed.
  • Orientation. The estimator enters the price as 1 - p, so that demand rises in every coordinate. That mirrors the pieces in p and changes nothing else.
